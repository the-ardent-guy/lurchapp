## SECTION 1 — Real Language from Reddit and Online Dater Confessions

### Bios and self-descriptions (what people actually write)

Dismissive-avoidant profile markers, per Psychology Today analysis of dating profiles (Arabi, Sept 2023):
- "Independent"
- "Free spirit"
- "Value my alone time"
- "Catch me if you can"
- "I want someone who doesn't make me feel trapped"
- "Nothing serious" / "Just looking" / "Not sure what I'm looking for"
- Lists of "musts" functioning as unconscious barriers: "must respect my alone time," "must have no baggage," "no drama"

From the broader pattern of real bios collected and categorized by Maroon App (datemaroon.com, 2026):
- "Former overthinker, current intentional dater"
- "Let's create a relationship where communication feels easy"
- "I'm the 'let's talk about life at 1 AM' type"
- "Love my career, gym, and peace. Looking for someone who won't disturb any of them"
- "Equal parts driven and goofy"

From a survey of dating app users (Arrow/Southeast Arrow, 2022, 82 respondents):
- People describe the bio as performing a version of themselves they're not sure they can sustain
- "84% of online daters want more authenticity" but produce idealized portrayals anyway

- Breadcrumbing: "drops morsels of flirtatious attention to keep the other person interested, even though they have no intention of participating in a relationship"

## SECTION 2 — Academic / Research Findings

### Meta-analysis: Dating apps and mental health

**Study:** "Dating App Use, Psychological Health, and Psychological Well-Being: A Systematic Review and Quantitative Meta-Analysis"
**Lead researcher:** Liesel L. Sharabi, Arizona State University
**Published:** Computers in Human Behavior, 2026
**Sample:** 26,000+ participants across 23 studies (2007–2024)

Key findings:
- Dating app users showed "significantly worse mental health outcomes, including depression, loneliness, anxiety, and psychological distress, than people who did not use dating apps"
- Effects were strongest for single users (vs. those in relationships who use apps)
- Effects were strongest in WEIRD nations (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic)
- Dating website users (non-app) showed no significant mental health difference — the swipe mechanic appears to be the variable
- Researchers note bidirectional causation uncertainty: people with worse mental health may gravitate toward apps, AND apps may worsen mental health; "probably, both things happen to some extent"

Second relevant systematic review: 45 studies (2016–2023) found most reported negative impacts of dating apps on body image; approximately half reported negative impacts on psychological well-being (ScienceDirect, 2024).

### Ghosting statistics

**Study:** Navarro, Larrañaga, Yubero & Víllora, University of Castilla-La Mancha, Spain
**Published:** Psicología y Salud (Redalyc), indexed study
**Sample:** N = 626 adults, 303 male / 323 female, ages 18–40, online survey

Key findings:
- **19.3%** (121 participants) reported experiencing ghosting in the past 12 months
- **23.2%** (145 participants) reported initiating ghosting in the past 12 months
- **35.6%** experienced breadcrumbing
- **36.7%** admitted to initiating breadcrumbing
- Breadcrumbing frequency exceeded ghosting frequency for both initiators and receivers
- Prior U.S. research cited in same study: 13–23% reported being ghosted; 11–29.3% admitted to ghosting

**Key finding on breadcrumbing vs. ghosting:**
Victims of breadcrumbing reported significantly more loneliness, helplessness, and lower life satisfaction than victims of ghosting. Ghosting alone showed no significant relation to the psychological correlates examined. The study was the first to establish this distinction empirically.

Source: Navarro et al., referenced in both PMC (PMC7037474) and Redalyc.

**Timmermans, Hermans & Opree (2021)**
*Journal of Social and Personal Relationships*, Vol. 38, No. 2, pp. 783–801
N = 328 mobile daters (63% female, 86% heterosexual)
Mixed methods (open + close-ended questions)
Findings: Ghosting has measurable impact on self-esteem and mental well-being among mobile daters; thematic analysis of motivations to ghost and coping strategies developed.

**BankMyCell survey (millennials 18–35, Tinder/Bumble/OkCupid/Hinge):**
- 82% of women have experienced ghosting on dating apps
- 71% of men have experienced ghosting on dating apps
- 50% of women who ghosted did so to avoid confrontation
- Over 1 in 4 men ghosted because a match didn't live up to their profile photos
- 1 in 6 men are likely to ghost someone they perceive as "clingy or needy"
- 25% of users report being ghosted after making plans

### Attachment theory on dating apps

**Attachment Project Study (attachmentproject.com)**
N = 44,435 participants (31,676 active dating app users)

Attachment distribution across Tinder, Hinge, Bumble:
- Anxious-Preoccupied: **44–45%** (most common across all three platforms)
- Secure: 35–37%
- Fearful-Avoidant/Disorganized: 14–16%
- Dismissive-Avoidant: 4–5%

Key finding: "People with the anxious attachment style are more likely to use dating apps and when they do, they are the most frequent users." Avoidantly attached individuals are less likely to use dating apps.

Highest secure scorers: Match.com and eHarmony (42% secure) — suggesting platform design selects for attachment style.

Users selecting "validation/confidence boost" as motive showed: lowest self-esteem, greatest emotion regulation difficulties, most insecure attachment patterns.

**Anxious attachment and dating app outcomes (Tandfonline, 2025):**
"Anxiously attached individuals crave closeness but fear abandonment, and the unpredictable nature of online dating mirrors that fear — a delayed response can feel like rejection, and a promising conversation that fades out can trigger panic or self-blame."

Anxiously attached daters commonly report: being told they "come on too strong" or are "too much too soon." Repeated ghosting experiences produce what researchers describe as a compounding wound — each ghost reconfirms the core belief that love will be withdrawn.

### Variable reward / slot machine psychology

From LSE Psychology Blog (2024), citing Wittmann et al. (2007, 2008), Bunzeck & Düzel (2006), Arias-Carrión et al. (2010), Schott et al. (2008):

The mesolimbic dopamine pathway is the primary neural system activated during dating app interactions. Two reinforcement mechanisms operate:

1. **Continuous reinforcement (onboarding):** Apps reward consistently for opening, establishing habitual return
2. **Variable ratio schedule (retention):** Intermittent, unpredictable rewards. Liu (cited in LSE) notes this "deliberate pacing creates a sense of anticipation" by staggering matched users. Variable ratio reinforcement is the strongest pattern of behavioral conditioning in psychology — the same mechanism behind slot machines.

Novelty alone produces dopamine release (Bunzeck & Düzel, 2006) — users receive reward simply from encountering diverse profiles, independent of matching. Neuroimaging confirms anticipation for novel experiences produces higher reward activation than familiar experiences (Wittmann, 2007).

Habitual swiping develops reflexive, automatic behavior, leading to "addictive usage patterns" (Narr, 2021, cited in LSE).

**Commodification of intimacy:**
Orchard, Treena. *Sticky, Sexy, Sad: Swipe Culture and the Darker Side of Dating Apps.* Aevo/University of Toronto Press, 2024.
Orchard (anthropologist, Western University) found: instead of advertised ease and fun, users encounter "endless upkeep, ghosting, fleeting moments of sexual connection, and a steady flow of misogyny." Her argument: platforms are "designed to make us swipe-dependent" rather than to produce relationship success.

From research synthesis: "The business model structurally depends on prolonged singlehood — a user who finds a partner leaves the platform."

Gyandeep Bhattacharyya writing in *Doing Sociology* (2026):
"When intimacy becomes something to be browsed, not built, we're left with a market that confuses volume for value, forgetting the fact that connection, at its core, can't be standardised, scaled, or sold."

From the *University of Melbourne Women's College Policy Unit*: "Disposable Desire: The Economics of Objectification in Dating Apps" — users are "well aware of the commodifying process at stake in dating apps and recognise the need to produce a desirable presentation of the self in order to 'stand out' from 'competitors.'"

### Relationship duration: met online vs. offline

- A 2024 demographic survey: ~34% of online daters had been in at least one relationship lasting longer than 6 months
- Online-origin marriages showed lower separation/divorce rates in earlier research (5.96% vs. 7.67% offline); however, more recent data from Psychology Today (2023) reverses this: online daters now report *less* satisfying and stable marriages than those who met offline
- Across countries, participants reported more intimacy, passion, and commitment if they met offline (IFS Studies)
- More than 50% of newlyweds in 2025 met on dating apps (Connected Couples, 2026) — but this is a meeting-method statistic, not a relationship quality statistic

### Missing archetypes — recurring types not represented in existing 10

**The Situationship Archaeologist (F or M, late 20s–30s)**
Not someone in a situationship — someone who has been in five and is now an expert in their own wound. Describes former entanglements with clinical precision but still repeats the pattern.
- Bio candidate: "We never put a label on it. We also talked every day for eight months. I'm still not sure what to call it."
- Wound: knows the language (situationship, talking stage, soft launch) but the knowledge doesn't protect from the experience

**The Returning Expat / The Settled-Enough (M, 32–38)**
Used travel, career ambition, or relocation as avoidance. Has now "arrived" — city picked, career stable — and downloaded the app because the environment for avoidance has been removed.
- Bio candidate: "Spent my 20s moving cities. Stopped. Thought the problem was geography. Download number one."
- Wound: confuses external stability with internal readiness

**The One Who Needs to Be Chased (F or M, late 20s)**
Becomes less interested when interest is returned. Pulls back when pursued. Intimacy tolerance, not intimacy desire, is the problem.
- Bio candidate: "I'm told I'm great until I'm not. I'm working on understanding the gap."
- Real language sourced from Cheryl Koo: "My unrequited crushes have always been unavailable men who do not give two shits about me. The appeal of him is precisely the fact that he only sees me through a professional lens."
- This type is currently split between Priya (avoidant) and Arjun (fearful-avoidant) without a character who owns it cleanly from the inside

**The Chronic Validator-Seeker (F or M, 25–30)**
On the app for matches as proof of attractiveness, not for dates. The match is the destination; the conversation is anti-climax.
- Bio candidate: "I respond fast and then don't know what to do with it."
- From Attachment Project data: users selecting "validation/confidence boost" as motive scored lowest on self-esteem and highest on emotion regulation difficulty
- Missing from current 10: all existing characters are looking for something. The validator-seeker is not looking for a person — they're auditing themselves.
